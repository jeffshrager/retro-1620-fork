# IBM 1620 Emulator with IPL-V: Ackermann and "The Raven"

## Part 1. Display Note

**A 1960 computer, running a 1950s AI language.** This exhibit is a working emulation of the IBM 1620, a small decimal computer that IBM introduced in 1959 and sold to universities, laboratories and engineering firms. Nicknamed "CADET" (a joke that it "Can't Add, Doesn't Even Try," because it added by looking up answers in a table in memory), the 1620 was the first computer many scientists and students ever touched. On it runs IPL-V, the Information Processing Language created by Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and Cliff Shaw, whose earlier versions powered the Logic Theorist (1956), often called the first artificial-intelligence program. IPL-V introduced ideas we now take for granted: linked lists, recursion, and dynamically allocated memory. Displayed alongside is a coding sheet in Newell's own hand, a direct trace of that tradition. The 1620 IPL-V interpreter itself was discovered by David Moews and digitized with Rupert Lane's GRIDLOCK OCR program, then corrected by hand; both programs run on a faithful software model of the machine.

**Ackermann.** The *Ackermann function* is a classic stress test for exactly the machinery IPL-V pioneered. It is tiny to state, yet it calls itself so deeply that it quickly exhausts any machine's stack. The program shown here is the one on the handwritten coding sheets on display: Allen Newell wrote it out on April 11, 1959, on an M.I.T. Lincoln Laboratory I.P.L.-V coding form, adapting an example from the TASS manual. The sheets carry two versions, one using integers and one that counts with lists of cells. Each line of the handwriting became a punched card, and the emulator runs those cards. Watch it compute Ackermann(3,3) = 61 through hundreds of nested calls; the answer is printed on the console typewriter when the computation finishes.

**Markov sentence generator (on *The Raven*).** This program does something closer to today's headlines: next-word prediction. It reads two stanzas of Poe's poem, counts which word follows which, and then generates new text by drawing each next word in proportion to those counts. It is a bigram Markov chain, a distant and very small ancestor of the language models of the 2020s, and it fits in 40,000 decimal digits of core memory, a fraction of what a single emoji occupies today. The generated "poem" is stitched from Poe's own word pairs, so it sounds familiar and is often grammatical for a line or two before it wanders. That is the point: nothing in the program knows what a word *means*, only what has followed it before. The program was written in 2026 by Jeff Shrager and Claude, an AI model from Anthropic, as a homage to the language's era, not a program from the period.

From a handwritten coding sheet in 1959 to a next-word predictor in 2026, both run on the same list-processing ideas, on a machine that is itself a software ghost of the original.

## Part 2. Historical Background

### The IBM 1620

IBM announced the 1620 in October 1959 and began deliveries in 1960. It was designed at IBM's San Jose laboratory as a low-cost scientific machine at a time when most computers filled rooms and cost millions. The 1620 rented for roughly a couple of thousand dollars a month, which put a computer within reach of a university department. About two thousand were built, and a generation of scientists, engineers and computer-science students learned to program on one.

The 1620 was unusual in several ways. It was a **decimal** machine with **variable word length**: numbers were strings of digits in core memory (20,000 digits to start, expandable to 60,000), each digit stored with a flag bit and a parity bit, and arithmetic could work on numbers of any length that fit in memory. It had no arithmetic hardware in the modern sense. Addition and multiplication were done by table lookup in a reserved region of memory, which gave rise to the "CADET" nickname (the IBM-internal joke being "Can't Add, Doesn't Even Try"). The later Model 2 (1962) added hardware arithmetic, faster core, and index registers. Input and output were by punched cards, paper tape and an electric typewriter that doubled as the operator's console. Software included SPS (Symbolic Programming System, an assembler), FORTRAN, and the student-friendly GOTRAN.

The 1620's low price and friendly scale made it a favorite for teaching and small research groups, and many early computing experiments outside the giant labs happened on it. It also became a platform for language experiments, among them the IPL-V interpreter shown here.

### Newell, Simon and Shaw: the road to IPL

In the mid-1950s, Allen Newell, Herbert Simon and J. C. (Cliff) Shaw, working at RAND and the Carnegie Institute of Technology, set out to build programs that could do what people do when they reason. To manipulate symbolic structures whose sizes could not be known in advance, they needed a way to build and rearrange them dynamically. The solution was the *list*: cells linked to other cells, allocated from a pool of free space as needed. They embodied it in a series of **Information Processing Languages** (IPL-I, II, and so on). The **Logic Theorist**, which proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica*, was demonstrated at the 1956 Dartmouth summer workshop, the event usually credited with naming the field of artificial intelligence, and was coded in IPL-II on RAND's JOHNNIAC computer.

IPL-V, the fifth version, aimed to make the language portable and teachable. Its second-edition manual, *Information Processing Language-V Manual* (Newell, ed., Prentice-Hall, 1964), documented a language of about 150 primitive processes, working on **lists**, **description lists** (attribute-value structures), and a **pushdown stack** that made recursion natural. IPL-V was implemented for a wide range of machines of the period, including IBM's 650, 704, 709 and 7090, and, in the version on display, the 1620. IPL is written at a very low level, close to assembly language, but its data model (symbols, lists, sublists, description lists) was a striking advance. John McCarthy's LISP, developed at MIT in the late 1950s, took up the list idea in a more mathematical form and became AI's dominant language; IPL is its slightly older sibling. Newell and Simon received the Turing Award in 1975, and Simon the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1978.

### The Ackermann function

Wilhelm Ackermann published his function in 1928, while studying the foundations of mathematics. It demonstrates that some functions are perfectly computable yet grow too fast to be built by ordinary bounded loops (that is, they are not *primitive recursive*). Defined in three lines, A(0,n) = n+1; A(m,0) = A(m−1, 1); A(m,n) = A(m−1, A(m, n−1)), it produces astronomically large values from tiny inputs, and it drives the depth of recursion sharply upward. That makes it a favorite benchmark for whether a system's stack and memory management are correct. A(3,3) = 61 requires many hundreds of nested calls, which is a genuine test for a 1620 running an interpreter. (The exhibit also carries a piece of hobbyist archaeology: getting this decades-old, OCR-digitized interpreter to run Ackermann correctly meant tracking down IBM's official modification letters and fixing card-deck errors. The emulator's author, Paul Kimpel, found that an original instruction (Transfer Numeric Fill) required odd addresses on the Model 2, and Rupert Lane found the IBM modification letters that address this.)

### "The Raven" as a bigram generator

Edgar Allan Poe's *The Raven* appeared in 1845, and its heavy, incantatory repetition ("Nevermore," "chamber door," "bust of Pallas") makes it well suited to statistical analysis. The technique used in the demo was first applied by the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov in 1913, when he counted vowel-consonant transitions in Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin*. Claude Shannon's 1948 paper, "A Mathematical Theory of Communication," produced approximations of English by choosing each word based on the previous word or two, and showed that a machine can generate text that looks like English by statistics alone. The "bigram" model is that idea at its simplest: to choose the next word, look only at the current one.

The program on display holds the poem's words and, for each word, a *description list* that maps each following word to the number of times it followed. Training walks the text once, updating the counts. Generation asks IPL-V's built-in random attribute selector (J16), which weights its choice by those counts, to pick the next word, then repeats. It is the same loop, at radically smaller scale, that underlies today's large language models: predict a next token from what came before, draw it from a probability distribution, append, and repeat. Where a modern model has billions of learned parameters and looks at thousands of preceding words, this one has about 80 words of vocabulary and looks at exactly one. It nevertheless shows, on a machine from 1960 with the language of 1963, that "predict the next word" is an old idea whose enormous recent success came from scale, not from a change of principle.

### About the emulator

The emulator is **retro-1620**, by Paul Kimpel, a browser-based model of the 1620 and its peripherals (console typewriter, card reader and punch, and control panel) that runs original 1620 software unmodified. The 1620 IPL-V interpreter (Wendell Beyer's original implementation) was discovered by David Moews, scanned and converted to text by Rupert Lane's GRIDLOCK OCR program, and then manually corrected before it ran faithfully. IBM's Modification Letters 3 and 4 were applied to fix the 1620 Model 2 punch-check problem. The Raven demonstration was written in IPL-V by Jeff Shrager and Claude, and its input is punched-card-style text, as it would have been in 1963.
